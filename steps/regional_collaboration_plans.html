Regional Collaboration Plans

Comparable-platform data from neighbouring Central Asian and Eurasian populations — evaluation, landscape, and priorities

Draft — 2026-09-20 Planning document, not yet contacted

1. The strategic question

Our pilot merge of the ALSU cohort with the public Kazakh GSA dataset and 1000 Genomes (see Reference Panels) produced results worth extending. The goal is not to maximize genome count, but to obtain comparable data — ideally array genotyping on the same or a closely related platform, so that merging does not introduce a technical batch effect that is confounded with population structure.

What "comparable" actually requires. Same array platform only removes one of four obstacles to a clean merge (the marker set). It does not remove differences in genome build/strand orientation, in genotype-clustering parameters (each lab's own .egt cluster file — an underrated source of batch effects), or in QC thresholds. The reliable path is to request raw intensity data (IDAT, or at minimum GTC + the cluster file used) and re-cluster and re-call all samples together under one shared QC protocol — not to treat "same chip" as sufficient by itself.

A WGS partner is not technically disqualified either — a full genome can be reduced to the array's site list (extract-by-position) and merged cleanly on common markers, with a manageable batch effect at common-variant frequencies. Platform match is a convenience, not a hard requirement.

2. Regulatory landscape (as of Sept 2026)

Russian Federal Law No. 43-FZ and Government Decree No. 750 entered into force 1 September 2026, regulating cross-border transfer of human genetic data. The regulation applies by the content of the data (any human genetic information), not by the technology used to generate it — array genotypes are covered the same as whole-genome sequence. Data from population-scale studies specifically face a stricter regime: transfer requires government commission approval and is permitted only in exceptional cases.

Practical implication for outreach to Russian institutions: requesting data transfer directly is likely to put a prospective collaborator in a position where they must decline. A federated-analysis model — our analysis scripts run at the partner institution, only summary statistics (allele frequencies, FST, PCA projection coordinates, ADMIXTURE Q-matrices) return to us — avoids this, and is standard practice in consortium GWAS meta-analysis. Individual-level genotypes never cross the border under this model.

Estonian Biocentre / Institute of Genomics, University of Tartu (Mait Metspalu) — long-standing chip-genotyping work on Central Asian populations, including Afghan Uzbek and Tajik samples (500+ individuals) and work on Turkic-speaking population history. Notably, the group has a documented prior collaboration with the Laboratory of Genomics, Institute of Bioorganic Chemistry, Tashkent — a warmer entry point than a cold approach. No Russian export restrictions apply.

4. Priorities and next steps

  1. Kazakhstan: extend the pilot merge to the full Kairov/Akilzhanova dataset; request IDAT + extended metadata; propose co-authorship on a joint regional paper.
  2. Estonia: approach via the existing Tashkent–Tartu institutional link, not cold; ask about historical Central Asian chip data and chip-generation harmonization expertise.
  3. Russia: propose the federated-analysis model explicitly in any first contact, rather than requesting data transfer.
  4. Compute the marker intersection between GSA-24 v3.0 (654,027, manifest A2), GSA MG v2 (665,608), and Infinium OmniExome (~4.5M) — a concrete number strengthens any outreach and pre-empts the first objection.
