Population Genomics of Uzbeks: Genome-wide Insights from a 1,047-individual Cohort

Manuscript draft — Population genetics of Central Asia (Paper 1 of 2)

Draft v0.1 · Apr 2026 Target: Genome Biology / Eur J Hum Genet / Genome Med ~5,000 words target

Title & Abstract

Working title

“Genome-wide characterisation of the Uzbek population reveals South-Asian/East-European European, South Asian and East Asian ancestry and low inbreeding in an array-based population-genetic survey”

Abstract (250 words, structured)

Background

Central Asian populations remain under-represented in human genomic resources. Existing reference panels (1000 Genomes, gnomAD) contain few or no Uzbek samples, limiting variant frequency estimation, imputation accuracy, and clinical interpretation in this population of ~36 million. Comparative studies place Uzbeks at the cline between European, South Asian, and East Asian ancestries (Yunusbayev et al. 2012; Jeong et al. 2019), but high-resolution autosomal data have not been published.

Methods

We analysed an expanded cohort of 1,256 quality-controlled, imputed Uzbek samples (custom Illumina array, imputed against TOPMed r2) together with 2,712 reference samples (EUR, EAS, SAS, AFR) of the 1000 Genomes high-coverage (NYGC 30x, GRCh38) release. We ran principal component analysis, ADMIXTURE (K=2–8), pairwise Weir & Cockerham FST for all ten population pairs, classical multidimensional scaling, the Population Branch Statistic (PBS), runs-of-homozygosity analysis and relatedness estimation. Findings were cross-checked with independent calculations (allele-frequency mixture fit, unascertained-SNP FST on chromosome 22, permutation and positive controls).

Results

Uzbeks form a cline between European, South Asian and East Asian reference groups. Weighted FST was 0.0143 against SAS, 0.0152 against EUR, 0.0400 against EAS and 0.1310 against AFR. At ADMIXTURE K=4 the Uzbek cohort was on average about 50% EUR-like, 30% EAS-like and 19% SAS-like (an allele-frequency mixture fit gave 49/28/23%); cross-validation error had no clear minimum, and at K=7 about half of the Uzbek ancestry formed a component absent from every reference. Self-reported ethnicity and birthplace were significantly associated with ancestry. A PBS scan of 74,171 SNPs found no candidate (maximum PBSUZB 0.070; Tier 1 requires 0.3), so no population-specific selection signal is supported in this panel. Median FROH was 0.0147, with 35 of 1,256 individuals above 0.0625; 0.078% of pairs exceeded PI_HAT 0.0884.

Conclusion

This work provides a medium-scale, openly described autosomal reference for Uzbeks, locates the cohort relative to global reference groups, and documents that an LD-pruned array panel does not support population-specific selection candidates. It also reports the limits of the approach (no Central Asian reference groups, array ascertainment of the SNP panel).

1. Introduction

Two narrative arcs to weave together: (1) Central Asia as a missing piece of the global genomic map, (2) the methodological case for adding Uzbeks specifically — geographic position, sample feasibility, biomedical relevance.

1.1. The Central Asia gap in human genomics

  • Population-genomic literature on Central Asia is dominated by Y-chromosome / mtDNA studies (Wells et al. 2001; Heyer et al. 2009; Karafet et al. 2018); autosomal SNP arrays are rarer.
  • Existing autosomal references include the HGDP (small Uyghur and Hazara samples; Bergstrom et al. 2020), Yunusbayev et al. 2012 (~600,000 SNPs across multiple Turkic groups, including a small Uzbek subset), and the SGDP (Mallick et al. 2016, single-digit Uzbek samples).
  • The 1000 Genomes Phase 3 release contains no Uzbek samples; gnomAD v3.1 reports no Central Asian super-population.
  • Consequence: imputation accuracy in Central Asia is reduced, allele-frequency estimates for clinical variants are extrapolated, and association studies cannot use a population-matched reference.

1.2. Why Uzbeks specifically?

  • Demographic weight: ~36 million people, the largest Central Asian nationality.
  • Geographic centrality: spans East–West Silk Road; a natural test case for ancient admixture.
  • Mixed ancestry expectation: prior work places them between EUR and EAS; this study quantifies the proportions and tests whether structure correlates with internal regional provenance.

1.3. Aims

  1. Provide a publicly described autosomal reference of 1,256 Uzbek samples for Uzbeks at imputation-grade SNP density (post-imputation 9.9M well-imputed variants).
  2. Position the Uzbek genetic background in global ancestry space using PCA, FST, and ADMIXTURE.
  3. Test for population-specific selection signatures using PBS with EUR + EAS outgroups (result: none supported).
  4. Quantify endogamy and relatedness via ROH and IBD.

2.2. Genotyping & QC

DNA from 1,247 self-identified Uzbek volunteers was genotyped on the [array name] following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quality control was performed in PLINK 1.9 (Chang et al. 2015): sample missingness > 0.20 (n=92 removed; 1,155 retained), IBD deduplication (PI_HAT ≥ 0.98 for monozygotic / sample duplicates; 57 removed; 1,098 retained), SNP call rate < 0.10, MAF < 0.01, HWE p < 10−6 (654K → 473K variants). See Step 1–Step 3.

2.3. Imputation

Eagle v2.4 phasing followed by Minimac4 imputation against TOPMed r2 was performed on the Michigan Imputation Server. Post-imputation we filtered on Rsq ≥ 0.3, MAF ≥ 0.01, HWE p < 10−6 in controls, retaining 9,981,814 SNPs across 1,074 samples; 16 samples were lost to ID-merge failures and the final analytic dataset comprised 1,047 individuals after population QC. See Step 4– Step 6.

2.4. Reference panel merging

Uzbek variants were intersected by chromosomal position and alleles with the 1000 Genomes high-coverage NYGC GRCh38 reference (3,202 samples, md5-verified; EUR, EAS, SAS and AFR used, 2,712 samples; AMR not used), restricted to biallelic SNPs, and LD-pruned (PLINK --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2, long-range LD regions removed) yielding 82,744 SNPs shared with the Uzbek data for FST, 64,959 for ADMIXTURE and PCA, and 74,171 after excluding 8,573 sites with an overlapping indel, multiallelic or structural-variant record for per-SNP FST and PBS.

2.5. PCA & ADMIXTURE

PCA used PLINK 1.9 --pca 20 on the 64,959-SNP merged data set (3,968 samples). ADMIXTURE 1.3 was run for K=2–8 with 5-fold cross-validation and 2 independent seeds. Component names were assigned from the mean membership of the reference superpopulations. Associations with self-reported nationality and birthplace were tested by Kruskal–Wallis with sample-ID-keyed joins, a label-permutation control and a positive control on the reference labels.

2.6. FST & MDS

Pairwise Weir & Cockerham FST (PLINK 1.9 --fst --within) was estimated for all ten pairs among five populations (UZB, EUR, EAS, SAS, AFR); the leave-one-chromosome-out jackknife standard error of the mean per-SNP value was computed; classical MDS in two dimensions was computed on the FST matrix. As an independent check, FST was recomputed on chromosome 22 from all common biallelic SNVs (PLINK for reference pairs, Hudson’s estimator from allele frequencies for the Uzbek pairs).

2.7. PBS & functional annotation

PBS (Yi et al. 2010) was computed with EUR and EAS as outgroups, treating Uzbek as the focal branch, with Tier 1 defined as PBS ≥ 0.3, Tier 2 as a minimum allele-frequency difference of 0.3 to every reference group and Tier 3 as near-private alleles. The highest-ranking SNPs were annotated by Ensembl overlap and variant lookup and by GWAS Catalog queries. A non-negative least-squares fit of Uzbek allele frequencies as a mixture of the references tested whether top-ranking SNPs are explained by admixture.

2.8. Runs of homozygosity & relatedness

ROH segments were called with PLINK --homozyg (window 50 SNPs, density 50 kb, minimum 1 Mb). FROH was computed as total ROH length / autosomal genome length (2,881,033 kb GRCh38). IBD/PI_HAT was estimated with PLINK on the LD-pruned dataset; pairs with PI_HAT > 0.0884 (loosely 3rd-degree relatives) were flagged.

3.1. Cohort overview

Of 1,247 individuals genotyped, 1,074 passed all QC and 1,047 entered the final population-genetic analyses (Table 1). Median age was [X] years; sex distribution and birthplace breakdown across 14 Uzbek administrative regions are given in Supplementary Table S1.

3.2. PCA & global FST

In the global PCA the first three components carry the structure (eigenvalues 214, 91, 29, then a plateau); PC1 separates AFR, PC2 separates EUR from EAS, and the Uzbek samples form a band along the EUR–SAS–EAS axis centred near SAS. Pairwise FST places UZB closest to SAS (0.0143) and EUR (0.0152), then EAS (0.0400) and AFR (0.1310); the UZB–EAS distance is less than half of EUR–EAS (0.0872). The ranking is unchanged on chromosome 22 using all common SNVs, although the absolute values of the array-based panel are smaller between Eurasian groups (for example EUR–EAS 0.087 vs 0.104), an effect of SNP ascertainment.

3.3. ADMIXTURE

Cross-validation error fell from 0.337 (K=2) to 0.322 (K=4) and then plateaued (0.3213–0.3219 for K=5–8); there is no clear minimum. At K=4 the reference superpopulations are almost pure (EUR 0.97, EAS 1.00, SAS 0.89, AFR 0.96) and the Uzbek cohort averages 0.50 EUR-like, 0.30 EAS-like, 0.19 SAS-like and 0.00 AFR-like. At K=7 about half of the Uzbek ancestry forms a component that no reference group carries, so the three-way description depends on K and on the reference panel (which has no Central Asian groups). Self-reported ethnicity (Kruskal–Wallis p < 10−40 for each component, with a permuted-label control behaving as expected) and birthplace among self-reported Uzbeks (p < 10−15) are associated with ancestry, but regional differences are small in absolute terms (regional mean EUR-like share 0.44–0.54).

3.4. PBS selection candidates

No SNP reached any tier. Of 74,171 SNPs, the maximum PBSUZB was 0.070 (mean -0.010, SD 0.020), which is where the bulk of the distribution places the maximum of that many values; the largest minimum allele-frequency difference to any reference group was 0.21. The 30 most differentiated SNPs between UZB and EUR all have Uzbek frequencies between the EUR and EAS values and are predicted by an admixture mixture model (r = 0.994 over all SNPs), i.e. they are ancestry-informative rather than selection signals. Earlier candidate lists (8 Tier 1 SNPs; a relaxed set of 490 enriched for HLA/MHC) came from batch-dependent genotypes in an older reference file and from extraction artefacts at indel-overlapping sites, and are withdrawn.

3.5. ROH & relatedness

44,938 ROH segments ≥ 1 Mb were detected across 1,256 individuals; median FROH was 0.0147 (mean 0.0188) and 35 individuals (2.8%) had FROH above 0.0625. FROH was weakly related to ancestry (Spearman ρ = −0.17 with the EUR-like and +0.17 with the EAS-like component). PI_HAT > 0.0884 was observed for 613 of 788,140 pairs (0.078%), 11 of them at second degree or closer, supporting the use of a nearly unrelated sample for GWAS. In a pre-QC merged array data set, PI_HAT-based relatedness lists were dominated by five poor-quality samples that are not in the final cohort.

4. Discussion

4.1. Position of Uzbeks in global variation

The FST estimates and admixture proportions reproduce the East–West gradient described in earlier sparse-marker studies (Yunusbayev et al. 2012; Jeong et al. 2019). Uzbeks are about equally close to South Asians and Europeans and clearly closer to East Asians than either of those groups is; which of SAS or EUR is closer is within the uncertainty of the panel and not a finding. Links to Indo-Iranian gene flow (Narasimhan et al. 2019) are plausible context but were not tested: the reference panel has no ancient or Central Asian samples.

4.2. Selection signatures

This array-based scan does not support population-specific selection in the Uzbek lineage. The panel is sparse (about 74,000 LD-pruned SNPs) and PBS is least sensitive for an admixed focal population, whose frequencies sit between those of its outgroups, so absence of a signal here is not evidence of absence. SNPs that rank highest by FST (including known pigmentation loci such as OCA2) are explained by the European/East Asian ancestry mixture. A selection analysis that models admixture, ideally on sequence data, is the logical follow-up.

4.3. Endogamy

Median FROH of 0.0147 is almost unchanged between the original and the expanded cohort, with a tail of 35 individuals above 0.0625 (parents at least first cousins in expectation). No pedigree or marriage data were analysed, so the tail is not attributed to endogamous customs. FROH weakly tracks ancestry, which should be modelled alongside it in downstream analyses; geographic stratification of FROH within Uzbekistan was not tested.

4.4. Limitations

  • Single-array genotyping introduces ascertainment bias (measured here: array-based FST between Eurasian groups is about 15% smaller than on all common SNVs); whole-genome sequencing of a subset is the obvious next step.
  • Recruitment from a limited number of clinical sites may not capture rural diversity.
  • Imputation against TOPMed (Anglo-American-dominant reference) inflates uncertainty for rare Central-Asian-specific variants.
